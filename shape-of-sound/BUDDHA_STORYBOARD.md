## The idea
Buddhism explained as **a chain of its own sayings, each one decoded**. Every saying stands in vertical gold columns,
read right to left, with a cinnabar seal naming its source. Under it comes a 「解」 line in plain words, and one visual
experiment that makes it click.

The frame is the Buddha's oldest metaphor for himself: **大医王**, the physician. 苦集灭道 is a diagnosis: the
illness, its cause, the fact that it can be cured, and the prescription.

The hook and the ending are **the two arrows** (《杂阿含经》卷十七). The first arrow is pain, and nobody avoids it.
The second arrow is what we add ourselves: 为什么是我, 完了, 凭什么. At the end the first arrow comes again, but the
thoughts turn to dust before they can become a second one. Last line: 痛，躲不开；苦，可以放下。

Through-line: a gold circle, 「你」, the target. It becomes the ensō behind 佛, the grip that closes into the hub of the
Wheel of Life, the chariot wheel whose spokes become the net of dependent origination, the mirror that isn't there,
the empty circle of the eighth Ox-herding picture, the moon, and in the end the target again, rising over the far
shore.

Look: **绀纸金字**, sutras copied in gold ink on indigo-dyed paper. Asymmetric layouts, vertical type, cinnabar seals,
留白. Chan turns to white paper and ink. Each tradition is shown through its own real art:
- Liang Kai, *Shakyamuni Emerging from the Mountains* (Southern Song)
- a Tibetan Wheel of Life thangka
- the Dunhuang *Diamond Sutra* of 868
- Sengai's ○△□ (Edo)
- Liang Kai, *The Sixth Patriarch Chopping Bamboo*
- the Ten Ox-herding pictures attributed to Shūbun (Muromachi)

The music travels the same way. India comes first: sitar, a tanpura-like drone, bansuri and low drums. China follows:
koto and dizi, with a 木鱼 pulse under the Heart Sutra. Then Chan, which is almost silent: a shakuhachi and 拍子木
clappers. At the end everyone joins, and the choir sings the syllable rhythm of the mantra up to D major. Leitmotif:
A–D–C–A (the arrow), answered by A–D–E–F♯–A–D.
